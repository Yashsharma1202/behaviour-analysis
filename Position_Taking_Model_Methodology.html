1. What the model actually is

This is an event-drift trading model: the observation that specific calendar-anchored events (a festival holiday, a quarterly earnings announcement, an RBI policy decision) are followed by a statistically repeatable price drift in a stock or index, over a short window of trading days before and after the event. The model does not predict the event itself (the holiday date, the RBI decision) — those are known in advance. It backtests what price behaviour has historically followed that known date, for each stock, and recommends a position only where that historical pattern is strong and has enough repeat occurrences to trust.

2. Step 1 — The position-taking window (T-n to T+m)

Every stock has its OWN window, expressed as T-n to T+m: enter n trading days before the event (T = the event's own trading-day anchor), exit m trading days after. This is not a fixed rule applied to every stock — it is chosen per stock, per event, as the window that historically produced the strongest, most consistent drift for that specific name. A tech stock that typically runs up 5 days before results is traded T-5; a bank that reacts mostly in the 2 days after is traded T+2 with a tight T-0/T-1 entry.

Where this window number comes from matters. For the 50 Nifty-50 stocks and the 4 major holidays, these windows were derived from dedicated historical optimisation runs (now part of the repo's data files). For the broader 211-stock F&O universe, the window comes from the same per-holiday backtest dataset (holidays_dataset.json). Windows are NOT re-optimised live on every run — they are a historical finding, re-used going forward. This is itself a modelling choice with a tradeoff explained in Step 2.

3. Step 2 — Direction selection (LONG vs SHORT)

For a chosen window, both directions are backtested on the exact same historical price moves:

DirectionRule
LONG winswhen the raw price move over the window is positive
SHORT winswhen the raw price move over the window is negative (exact mirror of LONG)

The direction shown is whichever had the higher win rate across all historical occurrences of that event for that stock. This is a best-of-both-directions, per-stock choice — not a single rule applied uniformly across the whole index.

This is the single biggest modelling tradeoff in the whole system, and it is disclosed on every report that uses it: picking the best-performing direction per stock fits the historical sample very closely (high in-sample win rates), but this project's own repeated out-of-sample testing has shown that a single uniform index-backed direction (e.g. always SHORT for Dussehra, regardless of per-stock history) has repeatedly outperformed the per-stock best-direction approach going forward. The per-stock approach is shown because it answers "what does this stock's own history say", not because it is a proven forward edge. Treat every LONG/SHORT direction in this system as the best historical fit, not a guarantee. (A more rigorous, walk-forward version of this same best-direction idea is used for the Nifty index options model — see Step 7.)

4. Step 3 — Split-robust return calculation

Within the T-n to T+m window, the return is NOT a simple (exit price − entry price) / entry price calculation. It is built day-by-day:

1

For each day inside the window, compute that single day's % price change.

2

If that single day's move is more than 20% in either direction, treat it as a stock split, bonus issue, or demerger artefact — not a real market move — and SKIP it (don't compound it into the return).

3

Compound all the remaining daily % changes together to get the window's total return.

year_return = ( ∏ (1 + daily_%%), excluding any day where |daily_%%| > 20% ) − 1, × 100

This fix was not theoretical — it was found necessary mid-project. DRREDDY's 5:1 stock split and RELIANCE's stock split both showed up as fake −80%-class single-day "crashes" in raw close-price data before this rule was added. Over 135 such corporate-action days were found project-wide. Every backtest number in this system has this correction applied.

5. Step 4 — Data sources & confidence tiers

Not every stock has the same depth of history. The system always discloses which tier a number comes from, rather than presenting a 1-year result with the same visual weight as a 26-year one.

Confidence flag: any stock with fewer than 3 years (n<3) of usable historical occurrences is marked LOW confidence and visually flagged (amber) everywhere it appears. A "100% win rate" built on 1-2 historical trades is one or two data points, not a statistic — it is shown, never hidden, but always labelled.

6. Step 5 — Entry/exit date computation

Once the window (T-n/T+m) and the event's anchor date are known, the actual calendar dates are computed by stepping across real calendar days and counting only NSE trading days (weekends and the 15 published 2026 NSE holidays excluded):

entry_date = shift_trading_days(anchor_date, −n)
exit_date = shift_trading_days(anchor_date, +m)

Where shift_trading_days walks one calendar day at a time from the anchor, decrementing a counter only on real trading days, until it reaches the requested count. This correctly skips weekends AND holidays that fall inside the window (e.g. a window spanning the Dussehra holiday itself correctly does not count that day).

For quarterly earnings specifically, the "anchor" is the company's own NSE-announced board-meeting/result date — pulled live from NSE's corporate-announcements feed, not estimated. Until a company files that announcement, its row shows "Yet to come" rather than a guessed date.

7. Step 6 — Position sizing (lot, margin)

Each recommendation includes the F&O lot size (exchange-fixed, per stock) and an estimated margin requirement, computed as a standard 20% of (lot size × current spot price) — a simplified SPAN-margin proxy, not the exact exchange margin (which varies with volatility and changes daily). It is shown to give a realistic capital-commitment estimate per position, not as an exact broker margin figure.

8. Step 7 — The 8x8 grid search (Nifty index options model)

The Nifty-index options backtests (the 1% ITM quarterly options master, the 12-rolling-quarters model) use a more rigorous, different method to pick direction and window than the per-stock method in Step 1-2 above — worth keeping distinct rather than conflating the two.

1

For a given quarterly event, test every entry offset 1-8 trading days before the anchor × every exit offset 1-8 trading days after — 8 × 8 = 64 window combinations, for LONG and SHORT separately (128 evaluations total).

2

Each combination is scored by (win_rate, avg_return), net of a 0.15% round-trip cost on the option premium (not a raw, cost-free price move).

3

The single best-scoring (window, direction) combination is selected — direction and window are chosen together in one search, not window-fixed-then-direction-picked.

4

A combination needs at least 3 valid prior observations to be considered at all; with zero prior history the model defaults to LONG, T-2/T+5 rather than leaving the choice undefined.

best(bo, so, side) = argmax over bo,so∈[1,8], side∈{LONG,SHORT} of (win_rate, avg_return net of cost), using only PRIOR quarters

The critical difference from Step 1-2: this is walk-forward, not in-sample. For quarter #12, the optimisation only ever sees quarters #1 through #11 — it re-runs fresh for every new quarter, using only what was knowable at that point in time, then is applied forward to the quarter it has never seen. This is genuine point-in-time validation. The per-stock holiday/Dussehra method in Step 2, by contrast, picks its best direction using ALL available years at once, with no held-out future data — which is exactly why the overfitting caveat in this document applies more strongly there than it does here.

9. Step 8 — Handling index reconstitution

When Nifty-50's membership changes (a stock added or removed), the model follows one additive rule: nothing is ever deleted.

1

The canonical membership flag (is_nifty50) is updated for the new and outgoing stock.

2

Historical records (past quarters, past executed positions) are left exactly as they were — a stock that WAS Nifty-50 at the time keeps its historical backtest untouched.

3

The outgoing stock is marked legacy in current/upcoming views (visually distinguished, excluded from "Nifty-50 only" filtered views) but its row stays, for F&O-universe visibility.

4

The incoming stock is added with its own real backtested stats (never a placeholder number) to every current/upcoming table it should appear in.

Known limitations — read before acting on any signal

LimitationWhat it means in practice
Per-stock direction overfittingBest-of-LONG/SHORT per stock has underperformed a single uniform index direction out-of-sample in this project's own testing. See Step 2.
Fixed windows, not re-optimised liveT-n/T+m values come from a historical backtest and are reused, not recalculated fresh every run — a genuine regime change in a stock's behaviour would not show up until the next full re-backtest.
Thin samples on newer stocksAnything flagged LOW confidence (n<3) is a real historical result, but statistically weak — treat with proportionally less conviction and size.
Win rate ≠ profitabilityThese are price-direction win rates, before transaction costs, slippage, and (for options) time decay. A 70% win rate does not by itself guarantee a profitable strategy after real trading frictions.
Margin figures are estimatesThe 20% SPAN proxy is a planning estimate, not the broker's exact margin requirement on the day.
EMA regime is descriptive, not predictiveIt tells you the current trend label, not where price goes next.
