NSE Behaviour Analysis · Methodology

Buy-Before / Sell-After
The logic, end to end

How the strategy positions around a corporate event whose date is already known — the two-leg trade, why you buy before and sell after, exactly when each leg fires, and how the before/after timing is chosen from history. Worked for Quarterly Results and Corporate Actions.

NSE India · Yahoo Finance prices (adjusted close) · in-sample study, not investment advice

01The idea: don’t predict the event — position around a known date

We never forecast whether a result or dividend will happen. We read the date off the regulatory calendar — it is a public fact — and predict only how the price tends to move around that fixed date, using the stock’s own history as the base rate.

Figure 1. The event date is an input we read; the only thing predicted is the direction of the move.

02The trade is two legs — buy before, hold through, sell after

One buy, one sell. You enter N trading days before the event at the closing price, hold straight through the announcement, and exit M trading days after at the close. You are never flat during the event.

Figure 2. The two legs. Buy-before catches the run-up; sell-after catches the post-event drift; the event sits in the middle.

03Why buy before, and why sell after

Buy before

To catch the anticipation

Because the date is public, traders position ahead of the catalyst and price tends to drift up into it — the “buy the rumour” run-up. Wait until the event day and that move is already gone. N is how far back you reach to sit inside the run-up.

Sell after

To catch the drift

The reaction doesn’t finish in one tick — prices keep drifting for several days as the market digests the news (post-event drift). Exiting on the event day sells into the first spike; holding M days lets the digestion play out.

Figure 3. Schematic price path. Buying at T−N and selling at T+M captures both the anticipation run-up and the post-event drift. Illustrative shape, not a specific stock.

04Exactly when each leg fires (from the code)

Anchor the event to a trading day T. T = the first trading day on or after the event date, so weekends/holidays snap forward to a real price day.
Buy leg — step back N trading days. entry = close[T − N]. Trading days, not calendar days, so “3 before” automatically skips the weekend.
Sell leg — step forward M trading days. exit = close[T + M].
Score the trade. Compute the return and label it a win or loss:
entry  = close[T − N]  // buy N trading days before, at the close
exit   = close[T + M]  // sell M trading days after, at the close
return% = ( exit / entry − 1 ) × 100
outcome = WIN if return% > 0  else  LOSS

06The two events — same machinery, different anchor date

Everything above is identical for both events. The only thing that changes is which calendar date T is pinned to, and how you know that date in advance.

Quarterly Results

T = broadcast date

Date column broadCastDate from financial_results.csv. Knowable ahead because SEBI LODR Reg 29 forces the company to intimate the results board meeting ≥ 5 clear days in advance — that public notice is your buy-before window.

Corporate Action

T = ex-date

Date column exDate from corporate_actions.csv. The ex-date and record date are broadcast by NSE (caBroadcastDate) days-to-weeks beforehand, so the date is fixed and public long before you must act.

Corporate actions use adjusted prices — the ex-date drop is already removed. Yahoo’s adjusted close back-corrects for dividends/bonuses/splits, so holding through the ex-date does not capture the mechanical price fall or a “recovery” of it. The corporate-action result measures the residual total-return drift around the ex-date, not dividend capture.

07How the before/after timing (N, M) is chosen — not by hand

We don’t guess “3 and 2.” We test every combination of N = 1…8 days before × M = 1…8 days after — an 8 × 8 = 64-rule grid — against all of the stock’s past events, then let the data pick the winner.

Figure 4. Each square is one “buy N before / sell M after” rule. All 64 are scored on the stock’s history; the highlighted cell is the one that gets displayed.

The selection rule

Require enough events. Keep only rules backed by roughly 5+ past events (or all of them if the stock has very few) — so the winner isn’t one lucky quarter.
Keep the profitable ones. Restrict to rules whose average return is positive (fall back to all if none are).
Rank by win rate, then average. The top rule’s N, M, win rate and average return become the displayed “buy N before / sell M after.”

08What the win rate means — and its limits

win rate = ( #events where return% > 0 ) ÷ ( total events ) × 100

A 70% win rate for the chosen rule means: across the stock’s past events of that type, buying-before / selling-after finished green on 7 of every 10. It is a historical hit rate, and the prediction for the next (known-date) event is simply that this base rate tends to repeat.

In-sample bias. The winning rule was chosen from 64 tries on the same history it’s scored on, so the number flatters. The true forward win rate is lower — read it as a description of the past, not a promise about the future.
Read it with the event count. 100% on 3 events is far weaker than 60% on 30. The number is only as trustworthy as the sample behind it.
The honest one-liner. Buy N trading days before a known event date at the close, hold through the announcement, sell M days after at the close — where N and M are the before/after pair with the best historical win rate. Buy-before harvests anticipation; sell-after harvests drift.
