Concept Note · Event-Driven Trading

Why “buy before / sell after” an event — the study behind it

The finance theory the Behaviour dashboard is built on: what makes a stock move around its corporate events, how you can know the event date in advance, and why the pattern is real in academic studies but usually arbitraged away on large, liquid stocks.

01It’s called an “event study”

The formal name for “how does a stock behave around an event” is an event study (Fama, Fisher, Jensen & Roll, 1969). The recipe is exactly what the dashboard does:

  1. Pick an event type — quarterly results, dividend, board meeting.
  2. Line up every past occurrence so day 0 = the event day.
  3. Measure each day’s abnormal return — the stock’s move minus the market’s move over the same days. (Raw returns are contaminated by the whole market rising or falling.)
  4. Average across all events to see the typical path: does the stock drift up before, react on, or keep drifting after the event?
The “buy N days before / sell M days after” rule is simply: find the window on that average path with the biggest gain.

02Two opposite forces move the price

There isn’t one reason a stock moves around an event — there are two, timed oppositely. This is the core concept, and the reason a naive grid search gets confused.

① BEFORE — Anticipation (“buy the rumour”)
For a scheduled event you know is coming, traders position ahead of time. If a good result is expected, buyers accumulate in the days before → price drifts up into the event. You buy 5–7 days before to ride that build-up.
② AFTER — Under-reaction drift (PEAD)
When news is genuinely surprising, markets under-react: the price jumps on the day but keeps drifting in the surprise’s direction for days–weeks as slow investors digest it (Ball & Brown 1968; Bernard & Thomas 1989). You buy after the surprise and hold.

03“Buy the rumour, sell the news”

This old market saying is the mechanism. By the time the news actually lands, the run-up has already priced it in, so the announcement is often a non-event or a reversal — the people who bought in anticipation take profits and the stock sells off. That is exactly why, on Reliance, the only honest profitable window was buy T-5, sell T-1 — get in for the anticipation, get out before the news, because the news itself tends to disappoint those already positioned.

Reliance, from our own data: +0.8% vs the market in the 5 days before results, then −0.6% in the 2 days after. A textbook run-up-then-reversal.

04How you “predict” the event day — you don’t, you read it

This is the make-or-break detail. You never guess the date; for scheduled events the law tells you, and for unscheduled events you simply cannot — which is what makes one tradeable and the other fiction.

Scheduled — date is KNOWN in advance
  • Results / board meetings: SEBI LODR Regulation 29 requires the company to intimate the exchange ≥ 5 clear days before the board meeting that considers results. So ~5 trading days out, the market knows the exact date.
  • Dividends: declared at that meeting; the ex-date & record date are set weeks ahead.
  • AGM, bonus, split: all announced well in advance.
Unscheduled — NOT predictable
  • Press releases, acquisitions, credit-rating changes, clarifications. Nobody knows these are coming.
  • So “buy 6 days before an announcement” is impossible — you’d need to know a press release lands next Tuesday.
  • In our audit, 81% of the apparent announcement edge lived in this un-tradeable pre-news window.

The tradeable timeline for a scheduled result looks like this:

05Why it’s usually not free money

The Efficient Market Hypothesis (semi-strong form) says: if “buy 5 days before results” reliably paid, traders would front-run it earlier and earlier until the edge is arbitraged to zero. On liquid, heavily-covered stocks like the Nifty 50, that is exactly what happens.

What our honest tests found on the Nifty 50:
  • The pre-event run-up exists but is tiny and eaten by costs.
  • Post-event drift (PEAD) is absent — beats didn’t even pop on day 1.
  • The “profitable” grid cells were mostly market drift (window-edge/corner solutions) or a date bug, not a real event effect.

06Where the effect actually survives

PEAD lives where information diffuses slowly: small, neglected, illiquid stocks — few analysts, slow investors, genuine under-reaction. That is the one place “buy the surprise and hold” works, and it’s why the promising direction is the wider small/mid-cap universe, not the efficient Nifty 50.

Rule of thumb: the edge you can capture ≈ how slowly the information spreads. Blue-chips price it in minutes; a thinly-traded small-cap can take weeks.

07Key terms

Abnormal return
Stock return minus the market’s return over the same period — the part attributable to the event, not the market.
Event window
The days around day 0 you study (e.g. T-8 … T+8).
Run-up / anticipation
Price drifting toward the expected outcome before a scheduled event.
Buy the rumour, sell the news
The run-up prices in the expectation, so the actual announcement often reverses.
PEAD (Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift)
The market under-reacts to a surprise; price keeps drifting in the surprise’s direction for days–weeks after.
Surprise
Reported result minus what was expected. The driver of PEAD — no surprise, no drift.
Intimation (SEBI Reg 29)
The mandatory advance notice of a board meeting — the reason a result date is knowable ~5 trading days ahead.
Efficient Market Hypothesis
Prices already reflect public information; any repeatable edge gets arbitraged away — strongest on liquid, well-covered stocks.
One-line summary. Buy-before harvests anticipation of a scheduled event (you know the date from the mandatory intimation); sell-/hold-after harvests under-reaction to a surprise (PEAD). Both are real academic effects — but on efficient large-caps they’re arbitraged away, so what a naive backtest “finds” is mostly market drift, and the announcement version is untradeable because you can’t know unscheduled news in advance.
